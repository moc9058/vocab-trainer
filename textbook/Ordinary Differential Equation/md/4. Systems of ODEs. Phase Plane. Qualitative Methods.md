# 1. Definitions

## 1.1. First-order system (§4.1)

A system of $n$ first-order ODEs has vector form

$$
\mathbf y'=\mathbf f(t,\mathbf y),\qquad
\mathbf y(t_0)=\mathbf K.
$$

## 1.2. Linear system (§4.2)

A linear system has the form

$$
\mathbf y'=A(t)\mathbf y+\mathbf g(t).
$$

It is homogeneous when $\mathbf g=0$ and nonhomogeneous otherwise.

## 1.3. Eigenvalue and eigenvector (§4.0)

For a square matrix $A$, $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue if there exists a nonzero vector $\mathbf x$ such that

$$
A\mathbf x=\lambda\mathbf x.
$$

The characteristic equation is $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$.

## 1.4. Fundamental matrix and Wronskian (§4.2)

Given $n$ solution vectors $\mathbf y^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf y^{(n)}$ of a homogeneous linear system, the fundamental matrix is

$$
Y(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\vert&&\vert\\ \mathbf y^{(1)}&\cdots&\mathbf y^{(n)}\\ \vert&&\vert\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The Wronskian is $W(t)=\det Y(t)$. If nonzero, the solution vectors form a basis.

## 1.5. Phase plane and trajectory (§4.3)

For a two-dimensional autonomous system, a **trajectory** is the curve traced by $(y_1(t),y_2(t))$ in the phase plane. A **phase portrait** depicts representative trajectories.

## 1.6. Critical point (§4.3)

A critical (equilibrium) point $\mathbf y_*$ satisfies $\mathbf f(\mathbf y_*)=0$. A solution starting there remains constant.

## 1.7. Stability (§4.4)

An equilibrium is **stable** if solutions starting sufficiently close remain close for all future times. It is **asymptotically stable** if, in addition, sufficiently close solutions approach the equilibrium as $t\to\infty$. Otherwise it is **unstable**.

## 1.8. Types of critical points (§§4.3–4.4)

For planar linear systems, typical types are **nodes**, **saddle points**, **spiral points**, **centers**, and certain degenerate or improper nodes. Classification depends on eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

## 1.9. Linearization and Jacobian (§4.5)

Near an equilibrium $\mathbf y_*$ of $\mathbf y'=\mathbf f(\mathbf y)$, the Jacobian matrix is

$$
J(\mathbf y_*)=\left(\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial y_j}(\mathbf y_*)\right)_{i,j}.
$$

The associated linearized system for $\mathbf z=\mathbf y-\mathbf y_*$ is $\mathbf z'=J(\mathbf y_*)\mathbf z$.

# 2. Theorems

## 2.1. Conversion of an ODE (§4.1, Theorem 1)

Every $n$th-order ODE

$$
y^{(n)}=F(t,y,y',\ldots,y^{(n-1)})
$$

can be rewritten as $n$ first-order ODEs by setting $y_1=y$, $y_2=y'$, ..., $y_n=y^{(n-1)}$:

$$
y_1'=y_2,\ \ldots,\ y_{n-1}'=y_n,\quad y_n'=F(t,y_1,\ldots,y_n).
$$

## 2.2. Existence and uniqueness (§4.2, Theorem 1)

For $\mathbf y'=\mathbf f(t,\mathbf y)$, if the components of $\mathbf f$ and their partial derivatives with respect to the components of $\mathbf y$ are continuous in a domain around $(t_0,\mathbf K)$, the initial value problem has a unique solution on some interval around $t_0$.

## 2.3. Existence and uniqueness for linear systems (§4.2, Theorem 2)

If all entries of $A(t)$ and $\mathbf g(t)$ are continuous on an open interval $I$ containing $t_0$, then

$$
\mathbf y'=A(t)\mathbf y+\mathbf g(t),\qquad \mathbf y(t_0)=\mathbf K
$$

has a unique solution throughout $I$.

## 2.4. Superposition (§4.2, Theorem 3)

For the homogeneous system $\mathbf y'=A(t)\mathbf y$, every constant linear combination of solution vectors is also a solution.

## 2.5. Eigenvector basis and general solution (§4.3, Theorem 1)

If a constant $n\times n$ matrix $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors $\mathbf x^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf x^{(n)}$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$, then

$$
\mathbf y(t)=\sum_{j=1}^n c_j\mathbf x^{(j)}e^{\lambda_jt}
$$

is the general solution of $\mathbf y'=A\mathbf y$.

## 2.6. Linearization (§4.5, Theorem 1)

For a planar nonlinear autonomous system with continuously differentiable components near a critical point, the Jacobian-based linearization determines the local type and stability in the nonexceptional cases specified by the text. The theorem notes exceptional cases involving equal or purely imaginary eigenvalues; these require additional nonlinear analysis.

## 2.7. Planar linear-system classification (§4.4)

For $\mathbf y'=A\mathbf y$, classification follows the eigenvalues of $A$:

- **Saddle:** two real eigenvalues of opposite signs; unstable.
- **Node:** distinct real eigenvalues of the same sign; attracting if negative, repelling if positive.
- **Spiral:** complex conjugate eigenvalues with nonzero real part; attracting if negative, repelling if positive.
- **Center:** purely imaginary conjugate eigenvalues in the linear system; closed orbits and stability without asymptotic attraction.
- **Repeated eigenvalues:** eigenvector structure matters; analyze separately.

These are classification criteria, not additional numbered theorems.

# 3. Solution Methods and Fundamental Formulas

## 3.1. Eigenvalue method (§4.3)

For $\mathbf y'=A\mathbf y$ with constant $A$, try

$$
\mathbf y(t)=\mathbf x e^{\lambda t}.
$$

This gives $(A-\lambda I)\mathbf x=0$. Find the eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and linear combinations of independent solution vectors.

## 3.2. Complex eigenvalues and real solutions (§4.3)

If $\lambda=\alpha+i\beta$ and $\mathbf x=\mathbf u+i\mathbf v$ is a complex eigenpair of a real matrix, real independent solutions are

$$
e^{\alpha t}(\mathbf u\cos\beta t-\mathbf v\sin\beta t)
$$

and

$$
e^{\alpha t}(\mathbf u\sin\beta t+\mathbf v\cos\beta t).
$$

## 3.3. Repeated eigenvalues (§4.3)

If there are too few independent eigenvectors, the solution method must account for the defective eigenstructure, yielding terms such as $t e^{\lambda t}$ in addition to $e^{\lambda t}$.

## 3.4. Phase-plane analysis (§§4.3–4.4)

For a planar autonomous system, locate equilibria, compute eigenvalues (or the Jacobian at equilibria), identify the trajectory geometry, and determine whether nearby trajectories approach or move away from each equilibrium.

## 3.5. Nonlinear qualitative analysis (§4.5)

For $\mathbf y'=\mathbf f(\mathbf y)$, solve $\mathbf f(\mathbf y_*)=0$, calculate $J(\mathbf y_*)$, and analyze its eigenvalues. When linearization is inconclusive, investigate the original nonlinear vector field and trajectories rather than asserting stability from the Jacobian alone.

## 3.6. Nonhomogeneous linear systems (§4.6)

For

$$
\mathbf y'=A(t)\mathbf y+\mathbf g(t),
$$

write $\mathbf y=\mathbf y_h+\mathbf y_p$, where $\mathbf y_h$ solves the homogeneous system and $\mathbf y_p$ is one particular solution.

## 3.7. Variation of parameters for systems (§4.6)

If $Y(t)$ is a fundamental matrix of $\mathbf y'=A(t)\mathbf y$, seek $\mathbf y_p=Y(t)\mathbf u(t)$. Then

$$
\mathbf u'(t)=Y(t)^{-1}\mathbf g(t),
$$

so a general solution is

$$
\mathbf y(t)=Y(t)\left[\mathbf c+\int Y(t)^{-1}\mathbf g(t)\,dt\right].
$$

# 4. Applications and Connections

## 4.1. Mechanical systems (§4.1)

Coupled mass–spring models yield simultaneous second-order equations that can be converted into first-order systems by introducing position and velocity variables.

## 4.2. Electrical networks (§4.1)

Circuit equations obtained from Kirchhoff's laws can be represented as linear first-order systems in suitable current or charge variables.

## 4.3. Nonlinear pendulum (§4.5)

The undamped pendulum equation is

$$
\theta''+\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta=0.
$$

Setting $y_1=\theta$, $y_2=\theta'$ gives

$$
y_1'=y_2,\qquad y_2'=-\frac{g}{L}\sin y_1.
$$

For small angular displacement, $\sin\theta\approx\theta$, producing a linearized oscillation model.

## 4.4. Population and interaction models (§4.5)

Nonlinear interacting-population equations illustrate how equilibrium points, trajectories, and linearization describe qualitative dynamics without requiring an explicit closed-form solution.
