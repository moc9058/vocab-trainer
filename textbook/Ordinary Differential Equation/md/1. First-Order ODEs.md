# 1. Definitions

## 1.1. Ordinary differential equation (ODE)

- An ODE is an equation involving an unknown function of one independent variable and one or more of its derivatives.
- Its **order** is the order of the highest derivative present.
- A first-order ODE can be expressed as $F(x,y,y')=0$; in explicit form, $y'=f(x,y)$.

## 1.2. Solution, general solution, particular solution

- A **solution** is a differentiable function $y=h(x)$ that satisfies the ODE on an interval.
- A **general solution** is a family of solutions containing an arbitrary constant $c$ (for first-order equations).
- A **particular solution** results from specifying a value of the arbitrary constant.
- A **singular solution**, when present, is a solution not obtained by selecting a constant in the general solution family.

## 1.3. Initial value problem (IVP)

- An IVP consists of an ODE together with a prescribed value at an initial point:

$$
y'=f(x,y),\qquad y(x_0)=y_0.
$$

## 1.4. Direction field (slope field)

- For $y'=f(x,y)$, the slope of a solution curve passing through $(x,y)$ is $f(x,y)$. Short line segments with these slopes form the direction field.

## 1.5. Separable ODE

- An equation is **separable** if it can be written as $y'=g(x)h(y)$, or equivalently $dy/h(y)=g(x)\,dx$ wherever division by $h(y)$ is valid.

## 1.6. Exact differential equation

- An equation $M(x,y)\,dx+N(x,y)\,dy=0$ is **exact** if a potential function $u(x,y)$ exists such that $u_x=M$ and $u_y=N$.
- The implicit solution is $u(x,y)=c$.

## 1.7. Integrating factor

- An **integrating factor** is a nonzero function $\mu(x,y)$ such that multiplying $P\,dx+Q\,dy=0$ by $\mu$ makes the resulting differential equation exact.

## 1.8. Linear first-order ODE

- A first-order equation is **linear** if it can be written as

$$
y'+p(x)y=r(x).
$$

- It is **homogeneous** when $r(x)=0$, and **nonhomogeneous** otherwise.

## 1.9. Bernoulli equation

- A Bernoulli equation has the form

$$
y'+p(x)y=g(x)y^a.
$$

- For $a\ne 0,1$, it can be reduced to a linear equation by substituting $u=y^{1-a}$ on intervals where the substitution is valid.

## 1.10. Orthogonal trajectories

- Two families of curves are **orthogonal trajectories** if their members intersect at right angles.
- At intersections with finite, nonzero tangent slopes, the slopes satisfy $m_1m_2=-1$.

# 2. Theorems

## 2.1. Exactness criterion (Sec. 1.4)

Suppose $M,N$ have continuous first partial derivatives on a suitable simply connected domain. Then $M\,dx+N\,dy=0$ is exact if and only if

$$
\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}.
$$

The textbook uses the equality of mixed partial derivatives as its exactness test; the domain condition clarifies the global converse.

## 2.2. Integrating factor depending only on $x$ (Sec. 1.4, Theorem 1)

For $P(x,y)\,dx+Q(x,y)\,dy=0$, if

$$
R(x)=\frac{P_y-Q_x}{Q}
$$

depends only on $x$ (where $Q\ne0$), an integrating factor is

$$
\mu(x)=\exp\left(\int R(x)\,dx\right).
$$

## 2.3. Integrating factor depending only on $y$ (Sec. 1.4, Theorem 2)

If

$$
R^*(y)=\frac{Q_x-P_y}{P}
$$

depends only on $y$ (where $P\ne0$), an integrating factor is

$$
\mu(y)=\exp\left(\int R^*(y)\,dy\right).
$$

## 2.4. Existence theorem for an IVP (Sec. 1.7, Theorem 1)

Consider $y'=f(x,y)$, $y(x_0)=y_0$. Suppose $f$ is continuous and bounded, $|f(x,y)|\le K$, in a rectangle

$$
R:\quad |x-x_0|<a,\qquad |y-y_0|<b.
$$

Then at least one solution exists locally, in particular on an interval $|x-x_0|<\alpha$, with $\alpha=\min(a,b/K)$ when $K>0$.

## 2.5. Uniqueness theorem for an IVP (Sec. 1.7, Theorem 2)

Under the preceding rectangle assumptions, suppose both $f$ and $f_y=\partial f/\partial y$ are continuous and bounded in $R$. Then the IVP has at most one solution there. Combined with the existence theorem, it has exactly one local solution.

# 3. Solution Methods and Fundamental Formulas

## 3.1. Euler's method (Sec. 1.2)

For $y'=f(x,y)$, an approximate solution with step size $h$ is generated by

$$
x_{n+1}=x_n+h,\qquad y_{n+1}=y_n+h f(x_n,y_n).
$$

This is a numerical approximation rule, not an existence or uniqueness theorem.

## 3.2. Separation of variables (Sec. 1.3)

For $y'=g(x)h(y)$, integrate

$$
\int\frac{dy}{h(y)}=\int g(x)\,dx+c.
$$

Check separately any constant solutions $h(y)=0$ that may have been lost by division.

## 3.3. Reduction using $y/x$ (Sec. 1.3)

When $y'=F(y/x)$, substitute $y=ux$. Then $y'=u+xu'$ and

$$
xu'=F(u)-u,
$$

which is separable when the right side allows separation.

## 3.4. Solving exact equations (Sec. 1.4)

Find a potential $u$ from $u_x=M$ and $u_y=N$, then write $u(x,y)=c$. One construction begins with

$$
u(x,y)=\int M(x,y)\,dx+k(y),
$$

where $k'(y)$ is determined by $u_y=N$.

## 3.5. Homogeneous linear first-order equation (Sec. 1.5)

For $y'+p(x)y=0$,

$$
y(x)=c\exp\left(-\int p(x)\,dx\right).
$$

## 3.6. Nonhomogeneous linear first-order equation (Sec. 1.5)

For $y'+p(x)y=r(x)$, let $P(x)=\int p(x)\,dx$. Then

$$
y(x)=e^{-P(x)}\left[c+\int e^{P(x)}r(x)\,dx\right].
$$

Equivalently, the integrating factor is $\mu(x)=e^{P(x)}$, and $(\mu y)'=\mu r$.

## 3.7. Bernoulli reduction (Sec. 1.5)

For $y'+py=gy^a$, let $u=y^{1-a}$, with $a\ne0,1$. Then

$$
u'+(1-a)p(x)u=(1-a)g(x).
$$

Solve this linear equation for $u$ and recover $y$. Check excluded or zero solutions separately.

## 3.8. Orthogonal trajectory equation (Sec. 1.6)

If the original curve family has slope $y'=f(x,y)$, the orthogonal family satisfies

$$
y'=-\frac{1}{f(x,y)}
$$

where the slopes are finite and nonzero. Vertical and horizontal tangencies require separate geometric treatment.

# 4. Applications and Connections

## 4.1. Exponential growth and decay (Sec. 1.3)

A quantity whose rate of change is proportional to its current amount satisfies

$$
y'=ky,\qquad y(x)=y_0e^{k(x-x_0)}.
$$

## 4.2. Newton's law of cooling (Sec. 1.3)

With constant ambient temperature $T_A$ and proportionality coefficient $k>0$,

$$
T'=-k(T-T_A),\qquad T(x)=T_A+(T_0-T_A)e^{-k(x-x_0)}.
$$

## 4.3. Logistic population equation (Sec. 1.5)

The logistic model has the form

$$
y'=Ay-By^2=A y\left(1-\frac{y}{K}\right),\qquad K=\frac AB,
$$

where $A,B>0$. For nonzero initial population, a solution can be expressed as

$$
y(x)=\frac{K}{1+ce^{-Ax}}.
$$

The equilibrium solutions are $y=0$ and $y=K$.
