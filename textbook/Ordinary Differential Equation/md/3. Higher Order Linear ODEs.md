# 1. Definitions

## 1.1. Higher-order linear ODE (§3.1)

An $n$th-order linear ODE in standard form is

$$
y^{(n)}+p_{n-1}(x)y^{(n-1)}+\cdots+p_1(x)y'+p_0(x)y=r(x).
$$

It is **homogeneous** when $r=0$, and **nonhomogeneous** otherwise.

## 1.2. Initial value problem (§3.1)

An $n$th-order IVP prescribes $n$ values at the same point:

$$
y(x_0)=K_0,\ y'(x_0)=K_1,\ \ldots,\ y^{(n-1)}(x_0)=K_{n-1}.
$$

## 1.3. Linear dependence and independence (§3.1)

Functions $y_1,\ldots,y_n$ are linearly independent on $I$ if

$$
c_1y_1+\cdots+c_ny_n=0\quad\text{on }I
$$

implies $c_1=\cdots=c_n=0$. Otherwise they are linearly dependent.

## 1.4. Basis, general solution, particular solution (§3.1)

A **basis** (fundamental system) consists of $n$ linearly independent solutions of the homogeneous equation. Its general solution is

$$
y_h=c_1y_1+\cdots+c_ny_n.
$$

Choosing specific constants gives a particular homogeneous solution.

## 1.5. Wronskian (§3.1)

For $n$ functions,

$$
W(y_1,\ldots,y_n)(x)=
\det\begin{pmatrix}
y_1&\cdots&y_n\\
y_1'&\cdots&y_n'\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
y_1^{(n-1)}&\cdots&y_n^{(n-1)}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

## 1.6. Characteristic polynomial (§3.2)

For the constant-coefficient homogeneous equation

$$
y^{(n)}+a_{n-1}y^{(n-1)}+\cdots+a_0y=0,
$$

the substitution $y=e^{\lambda x}$ yields

$$
P(\lambda)=\lambda^n+a_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0=0.
$$

## 1.7. Complementary and particular solutions (§3.3)

For $Ly=r$, $y_h$ is the general solution of $Ly=0$ and $y_p$ is one solution of $Ly=r$. The general nonhomogeneous solution is $y=y_h+y_p$.

# 2. Theorems

## 2.1. Fundamental theorem for homogeneous linear ODEs (§3.1, Theorem 1)

Sums and constant multiples of homogeneous solutions on an open interval $I$ are again solutions on $I$ (superposition principle).

## 2.2. Existence and uniqueness for IVPs (§3.1, Theorem 2)

If $p_0,\ldots,p_{n-1}$ are continuous on an open interval $I$ containing $x_0$, the homogeneous $n$th-order equation together with prescribed initial data $y(x_0),\ldots,y^{(n-1)}(x_0)$ has exactly one solution on $I$.

## 2.3. Wronskian criterion (§3.1, Theorem 3)

For $n$ solutions of a homogeneous linear ODE with continuous coefficients on $I$, the solutions are linearly dependent if and only if their Wronskian is zero at some point of $I$. If it vanishes at one point, it vanishes everywhere on $I$; if nonzero at one point, the solutions form a basis.

## 2.4. Existence of a general solution (§3.1, Theorem 4)

An $n$th-order homogeneous linear ODE with continuous coefficients on $I$ has a general solution consisting of $n$ linearly independent solutions.

## 2.5. General solution includes all solutions (§3.1, Theorem 5)

Under the same continuity conditions, every solution is a linear combination of a basis. Thus no additional singular solutions occur.

## 2.6. Distinct-root basis (§3.2, Theorem 1)

For a constant-coefficient homogeneous equation, the exponential solutions $e^{\lambda_1x},\ldots,e^{\lambda_nx}$ form a basis precisely when the $n$ characteristic roots are distinct.

## 2.7. Independence of exponential solutions (§3.2, Theorem 2)

Exponentials corresponding to distinct characteristic roots are linearly independent on every open interval.

# 3. Solution Methods and Fundamental Formulas

## 3.1. Distinct real characteristic roots (§3.2)

If the characteristic polynomial has distinct real roots $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$,

$$
y_h=\sum_{j=1}^n c_je^{\lambda_jx}.
$$

## 3.2. Repeated real roots (§3.2)

For a real root $\lambda$ of multiplicity $m$, the associated independent solutions are

$$
e^{\lambda x},\ xe^{\lambda x},\ldots,x^{m-1}e^{\lambda x}.
$$

## 3.3. Complex roots (§3.2)

For real coefficients, a conjugate pair $\alpha\pm i\beta$ produces real solutions

$$
e^{\alpha x}\cos(\beta x),\qquad e^{\alpha x}\sin(\beta x).
$$

For multiplicity $m$, multiply both forms by $1,x,\ldots,x^{m-1}$.

## 3.4. Higher-order Euler–Cauchy equations (§3.1)

For equations with derivative coefficients proportional to powers of $x$, try $y=x^m$ on $x>0$. Substitution produces a polynomial equation in $m$. Repeated roots lead to powers of $\ln x$, analogously to repeated exponential roots.

## 3.5. Nonhomogeneous solution (§3.3)

For $Ly=r$, first find $y_h$ from $Ly=0$ and then a particular solution $y_p$:

$$
y=y_h+y_p.
$$

## 3.6. Undetermined coefficients (§3.3)

For constant-coefficient equations with forcing made from polynomials, exponentials, and trigonometric functions, select a trial form of the corresponding type and determine its coefficients by substitution. Multiply by an adequate power of $x$ if the trial form overlaps with the complementary solution.

## 3.7. Variation of parameters (§3.3)

For an $n$th-order equation in standard form, with fundamental solutions $y_1,\ldots,y_n$, seek

$$
y_p=\sum_{j=1}^n u_j(x)y_j(x).
$$

Choose $u_j'$ from the linear system

$$
\sum_j u_j'y_j^{(k)}=0\quad(k=0,\ldots,n-2),\qquad
\sum_j u_j'y_j^{(n-1)}=r(x).
$$

The coefficient matrix is the Wronskian matrix and is invertible for a basis. Integrate $u_j'$ to obtain $y_p$.

# 4. Applications and Connections

- The homogeneous solution space has dimension $n$.
- The Wronskian links functional independence to solvability of the initial-value linear system.
- Characteristic-root multiplicities determine the number of $x^ke^{\lambda x}$ factors.
- The complementary-plus-particular decomposition extends the second-order theory of Chapter 2 to arbitrary order.
