# 9. Multilinear Algebra and Determinants

> Throughout this chapter, all vector spaces are finite-dimensional over $F=\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$, $n=\dim V\geq1$, and $m\geq1$ is an integer. Bases used for matrices are ordered. Bilinear means linear in both variables, including over $\mathbb C$; it is different from a complex inner product. The tensor-product construction using multilinear forms on dual spaces below uses finite-dimensionality. Inner products are linear in the first variable.

# 1. Definitions

## 1.1. Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms

### 1.1.1. <span style="color: red">(Bilinear form)</span> A bilinear form is a function $\beta : V \times V \rightarrow F$ such that

$$
v\mapsto \beta(v,u)\ \text{and}\ v \mapsto \beta(u,v)
$$

are both linear functionals on $V$ for every $u \in V$. The set of bilinear forms is denoted by $V^{(2)}$.

- A bilinear form $\beta$ is symmetric if $\beta(u,v)=\beta(v,u)$ for all $u,v\in V$. Their space is denoted by $V_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}$.
- The space of alternating bilinear forms is denoted by $V_{\mathrm{alt}}^{(2)}$
  - $\alpha \in V_{\mathrm{alt}}^{(2)}$ if $\alpha(v,v)=0$ for every $v\in V$

### 1.1.2. <span style="color: red">(Matrix representation of bilinear form)</span> Suppose $\beta$ is a bilinear form and let $\mathscr{B}=(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$ be an ordered basis of $V$. The matrix representation is defined as $M_{\mathscr{B}}(\beta)_{i,j}=\beta(e_i,e_j)$.

- Note that $\beta \mapsto M_{\mathscr{B}}(\beta)$ is an isomorphism.

### 1.1.3. <span style="color: red">(Quadratic form)</span> For $\beta \in V^{(2)}$, define $q_\beta:V \rightarrow F$ by $q_\beta(v)=\beta(v,v)$. A function $q:V\to F$ is called a quadratic form on $V$ if there exists $\beta$ such that $q=q_\beta$.

## 1.2. Alternating Multilinear Forms

### 1.2.1. <span style="color: red">(Multilinear form)</span> An $m$-linear form on $V$ is a function $\alpha:V^m\to F$ that is linear in each variable while the other variables are fixed. The vector space of these forms is denoted by $V^{(m)}$.

<ul>
<li>
$\alpha \in V_{\mathrm{alt}}^{(m)}$: $\alpha(v_1,\ldots,v_m)=0$ if $v_j=v_k$ for some $j \neq k$
</li>
</ul>

### 1.2.2. <span style="color: red">(Permutation)</span> Let $S_m$ denote the permutations of $\{1,\ldots,m\}$, and let $\sigma\in S_m$. Then

$$
\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)=(-1)^{N}
$$

where $N$ is the number of pairs of integers $(k,l)$ with $1 \leq k < l \leq m$ such that $k$ appears after $l$ in the list $\sigma(1),\ldots,\sigma(m)$.

## 1.3. Determinants

### 1.3.1. <span style="color: red">(Determinant)</span> Let $n=\dim V\geq1$. For $\alpha\in V_{\mathrm{alt}}^{(n)}$ and $T\in\mathcal L(V)$, define

$$
\alpha_T(v_1,\ldots,v_n)=\alpha(Tv_1,\ldots,Tv_n).
$$

Define $\det T$ to be the coefficient such that

$$
\alpha_T=(\det T)\alpha,\ \forall \alpha\in V_{\mathrm{alt}}^{(n)}.
$$

For $A \in F^{n \times n}$, define $\det A=\det T$ for $T \in \mathcal{L}(F^n)$ whose matrix representation with respect to the standard basis is equal to $A$.

- The map $\alpha\mapsto\alpha_T$ is a linear endomorphism of the one-dimensional space $V_{\mathrm{alt}}^{(n)}$. Hence it is multiplication by a unique scalar.

### 1.3.2. <span style="color: red">(Characteristic polynomial)</span> Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. The polynomial defined as follows is called the characteristic polynomial.

$$
z \mapsto \det(zI-T)
$$

## 1.4. Tensor Products

### 1.4.1. Let $\mathcal{B}(V_1,\ldots,V_m)$ denote the set of all $m$-linear forms $\beta:V_1 \times \cdots \times V_m \rightarrow F$.

### 1.4.2. <span style="color: red">(Tensor product)</span> Let $V_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes V_m:=\mathcal{B}(V_1',\ldots,V_m')$. The tensor product $v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_m \in \mathcal{B}(V_1',\ldots,V_m')$ is defined as follows

$$
(v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_m)(\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_m )=\phi_1(v_1)\cdots\phi_m(v_m).
$$

- Note that $\dim(V_1\otimes \cdots \otimes V_m)=\dim(V_1)\cdots \dim(V_m)$

### 1.4.3. <span style="color: red">(Inner product of tensor product)</span> Suppose $V_1,\ldots,V_m$ are inner product spaces. Then define the inner product on $V_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes V_m$ as the one that satisfies the following:

$$
\langle v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_m, u_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes u_m \rangle=\langle v_1,u_1 \rangle \cdots \langle v_m, u_m\rangle
$$

- This prescription extends sesquilinearly from pure tensors and defines a unique positive-definite inner product; existence and positivity follow by using tensor products of orthonormal bases.
- Note that $\|v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_m\|=\|v_1\|\cdots\|v_m\|$

# 2. Theorems

## 2.1. Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms

### 2.1.1. Suppose $\beta$ is a bilinear form on $V$ and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Define bilinear forms $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ on $V$ by

$$
\alpha(u,v)=\beta(u,Tv)\ \text{and}\ \gamma(u,v)=\beta(Tu,v).
$$

Let $\mathscr{B}$ be a basis of $V$. Then

$$
M_{\mathscr{B}}(\alpha)=M_{\mathscr{B}}(\beta)[T]_{\mathscr B}\ \text{and}\ M_{\mathscr{B}}(\gamma)=[T]_{\mathscr B}^{\mathsf T}M_{\mathscr{B}}(\beta).
$$

### 2.1.2. <span style="color: red">(Change of basis formula)</span> Suppose $\beta \in V^{(2)}$. Suppose $\mathscr{B}=\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\},\ \mathscr{C}=\{f_1,\ldots,f_n\}$ are bases of $V$. Let

$$
A=M_{\mathscr{B}}(\beta),\ B=M_{\mathscr{C}}(\beta)\ \text{and}\ C=[I]_{\mathscr{B}}^{\mathscr{C}}.
$$

Then $A=C^{\mathsf T}BC$.

- Bilinear-form matrices transform by congruence, $A=C^{\mathsf T}BC$, whereas operator matrices transform by similarity. For a real orthogonal change matrix, $C^{\mathsf T}=C^{-1}$, so the two transformation rules have the same algebraic form.

### 2.1.3. Suppose $\rho \in V^{(2)}$. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1) $\rho$ is a symmetric bilinear form on $V$

(2) $M_{\mathscr{B}}(\rho)$ is a symmetric matrix for every basis $\mathscr{B}$ of $V$

(3) $M_{\mathscr{B}}(\rho)$ is a symmetric matrix for some basis $\mathscr{B}$ of $V$

(4) $M_{\mathscr{B}}(\rho)$ is a diagonal matrix for some basis $\mathscr{B}$ of $V$

### 2.1.4. Suppose $F=\mathbb{R}$ and $V$ is an inner product space. Let $\rho \in V_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}$. Then there exists an orthonormal basis $\mathscr{B}$ where $M_{\mathscr{B}}(\rho)$ is diagonal.

### 2.1.5. $V^{(2)}=V_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}\oplus V_{\mathrm{alt}}^{(2)}$.

### 2.1.6. Suppose $V=F^n$. $q$ is a quadratic form on $V$ if and only if there exists $A \in F^{n \times n}$ such that

$$
q(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n A_{i,j}x_ix_j.
$$

### 2.1.7. Suppose $q:V\to F$. Then $q$ is a quadratic form if and only if there exists a unique $\rho \in V_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}$ such that $q = q_\rho$.

### 2.1.8. Suppose $q$ is a quadratic form. Then

(1) There exists a basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ of $V$ and $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n \in F$ such that

$$
q(x_1e_1+\cdots+x_ne_n)=\lambda_1 x_1^2 + \cdots + \lambda_n x_n^2
$$

(2) If $F=\mathbb{R}$ and $V$ is an inner product space, then the basis in (1) can be chosen to be an orthonormal basis of $V$

## 2.2. Alternating Multilinear Forms

### 2.2.1. Suppose $\alpha \in V_{\mathrm{alt}}^{(m)}$ and $v_1,\ldots,v_m$ are linearly dependent. Then

$$
\alpha(v_1,\ldots,v_m)=0
$$

- If $m > \dim(V)$, then $0$ is the only alternating $m$-linear form

### 2.2.2. Suppose $\alpha \in V_{\mathrm{alt}}^{(m)}$. For every $\sigma\in S_m$ and $v_1,\ldots,v_m\in V$,

$$
\alpha(v_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,v_{\sigma(m)})=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\alpha(v_1,\ldots,v_m)
$$

- Note that swapping two entries in a permutation multiplies the sign of the permutation by $-1$.

### 2.2.3. Let $n=\dim(V)$. Let $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ be a basis of $V$, $\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_n$ be the dual basis and $v_1,\ldots,v_n \in V$. Then

$$
\alpha(v_1,\ldots,v_n)=\alpha(e_1,\ldots,e_n)\sum_{\sigma \in S_n}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\varphi_{\sigma(1)}(v_1)\cdots\varphi_{\sigma(n)}(v_n)
$$

for every $\alpha \in V_{\mathrm{alt}}^{(n)}$.

- Note that $\dim(V_{\mathrm{alt}}^{(n)})=1$.

### 2.2.4. Suppose $n=\dim(V),\alpha \in V_{\mathrm{alt}}^{(n)}$ is nonzero and $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ is a list of vectors in $V$. Then $\alpha(e_1,\ldots,e_n) \neq 0$ if and only if $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ is linearly independent.

## 2.3. Determinants

### 2.3.1. The map $(v_1,\ldots,v_n) \mapsto \det(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$ is an alternating $n$-linear form on $F^n$.

### 2.3.2. Let $A \in F^{n \times n}$. Then

$$
\det A = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)A_{\sigma(1),1}\cdots A_{\sigma(n),n}.
$$

- Note that if $A$ is upper-triangular with $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$ on the diagonal, then $\det A = \lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n$

### 2.3.3. <span style="color: red">(Properties of determinants)</span> Suppose $S,T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and $A,B \in F^{n \times n}$.

(1) $\det ST = \det S \cdot \det T$ and $\det AB = \det A \cdot \det B$.

(2) $T$ is invertible if and only if $\det T \neq 0$. Furthermore, if $T$ is invertible, then

$$
\det T^{-1}=\cfrac{1}{\det T}.
$$

- $A$ is invertible if and only if $\det A \neq 0$.

(3) Let $S \in \mathcal{L}(W,V)$ be invertible. Then

$$
\det(S^{-1}TS)=\det(T)
$$

- Note that $\det T = \det [T]_{\mathscr{B}}$ for any basis $\mathscr{B}$.
- Suppose $F = \mathbb{C}$. Then $\det T=\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n$, where the eigenvalues are listed with algebraic multiplicity.

(4) $\det T=\det T'$ and $\det A=\det A^{\mathsf T}$. If $V$ is an inner product space, $\det T^*=\overline{\det T}$.

(5) If $V$ is an inner product space, $|\det T|=\sqrt{\det(T^*T)}=\text{product of singular values of }T$.

### 2.3.4. Suppose $V$ is an inner product vector space.

(1) If $S \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ is unitary, then $|\det S|=1$.

(2) If $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ is positive, then $\det T \geq 0$.

### 2.3.5. Suppose $F=\mathbb{C}$. Let $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m$ be distinct eigenvalues of $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and let $d_1,\ldots,d_m$ denote their multiplicities. Then

$$
\det(zI-T)=(z-\lambda_1)^{d_1}\cdots(z-\lambda_m)^{d_m}.
$$

### 2.3.6. <span style="color: red">(Cayley-Hamilton theorem)</span> Let $q$ be the characteristic polynomial of $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. Then $q(T)=0$.

### 2.3.7. The characteristic polynomial of $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ can be written as

$$
z^n - \operatorname{tr}(T)z^{n-1} + \cdots + (-1)^{n}(\det T).
$$

### 2.3.8. <span style="color: red">(Hadamard’s inequality)</span> Suppose $A\in F^{n \times n}$. Then

$$
|\det A| \leq \prod_{k=1}^n\|A_{\cdot,k}\|
$$

### 2.3.9. <span style="color: red">(Vandermonde matrix)</span> Let $\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_n \in F$. Then

$$
\det \begin{pmatrix}
1 & \beta_1 & \beta_1^2 & \cdots & \beta_1^{n-1}\\
1 & \beta_2 & \beta_2^2 & \cdots & \beta_2^{n-1}\\
\\
 & & \ddots & & \\
\\
1 & \beta_n & \beta_n^2 & \cdots & \beta_n^{n-1}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\prod_{1\leq j < k \leq n}(\beta_k - \beta_j).
$$

## 2.4. Tensor Products

### 2.4.1. $(v_1,\ldots,v_m)\mapsto v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_m$ is $m$-linear.

### 2.4.2. Suppose $\beta_i =\{e_{i,1},\ldots,e_{i,n_i}\}$ is a linearly independent list in $V_i$ for each $i$, $i=1,\ldots,m$. Then

$$
\alpha=\{e_{1,j_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e_{m,j_m}\}_{j_1=1,\ldots,n_1,\ldots,j_m=1,\ldots,n_m}
$$

is a linearly independent subset of $V_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes V_m$. Furthermore, if $\beta_i$’s are bases, then so is $\alpha$.

### 2.4.3. Suppose $\Gamma : V_1 \times \cdots \times V_m \rightarrow U$ is an $m$-linear map. Then there exists a unique linear map $\hat{\Gamma}:V_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes V_m \rightarrow U$ such that

$$
\hat{\Gamma}(v_1\otimes\cdots\otimes v_m)=\Gamma(v_1,\ldots,v_m).
$$

Conversely, suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes V_m,U)$. Then there exists a unique $m$-linear map $T^{\#}:V_1 \times \cdots \times V_m \rightarrow U$ such that

$$
T^{\#}(v_1,\ldots,v_m)=T(v_1 \otimes  \cdots \otimes v_m).
$$

### 2.4.4. Suppose $V$ and $W$ are inner product spaces, and let $e_1,\ldots,e_m$ and $f_1,\ldots,f_n$ be orthonormal bases of $V$ and $W$, respectively. Then

$$
\{e_i\otimes f_j:1\leq i\leq m,\ 1\leq j\leq n\}
$$

is an orthonormal basis of $V \otimes W$.
