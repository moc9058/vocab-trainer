# 1. Definitions

## 1.1. Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators

### 1.1.1. <span style="color: red">(Adjoint operator)</span> Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)$. The <b>adjoint</b> of $T$ is the function $T^*:W\rightarrow V$ such that

$$
\langle Tv, w\rangle_W = \langle v, T^*w \rangle_V
$$

for every $v\in V$ and $w \in W$.

- It can be easily shown that $T^*$ exists uniquely and is linear, by the finite-dimensional Riesz representation theorem.

### 1.1.2. <span style="color: red">(Conjugate transpose)</span> Let $A\in F^{m\times n}$. Then the <b>conjugate transpose</b> $A^*$ of $A$ is defined as follows.

$$
\big(A^*\big)_{i,j}=\overline{A_{j,i}}
$$

### 1.1.3. <span style="color: red">(Self-adjoint) </span>An operator $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is called <b>self-adjoint</b> if $T=T^*$.

### 1.1.4. <span style="color: red">(Normal)</span> An operator $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is called <b>normal</b> if $TT^*=T^*T$.

### 1.1.5. <span style="color: red">(Skew-adjoint)</span> An operator $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is called skew-adjoint if $T^*=-T$.

## 1.2. Spectral Theorem

An orthonormal eigenbasis is an orthonormal basis consisting of eigenvectors of an operator. The real and complex spectral theorems in §2.2 characterize when such a basis exists.

## 1.3. Positive Operators

### 1.3.1. <span style="color: red">(Positive operator)</span> An operator $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is called positive if it is self-adjoint and

$$
\langle Tv,v \rangle \geq 0,\ v\in V.
$$

- For a positive operator $T$, invertibility is equivalent to $\langle Tv,v\rangle>0$ for every nonzero $v\in V$. Over $\mathbb C$, the condition $\langle Tv,v\rangle\geq0$ for all $v$ already implies self-adjointness.
- A matrix $B \in F^{n \times n}$ is called <b>positive definite</b> if $B^*=B$ and $\langle Bx,x \rangle>0$ for all nonzero $x \in F^n$.

### 1.3.2. An operator $R$ is a square root of an operator $T$ if $R^2=T$. For a positive operator, $\sqrt{T}$ denotes the unique positive square root of $T$.

## 1.4. Isometrics, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization

### 1.4.1. <span style="color: red">(Isometry)</span> A linear map $S \in \mathcal{L}(V,W)$ is called an isometry if

$$
\|Sv\|=\|v\|,\ v\in V.
$$

### 1.4.2. <span style="color: red">(Unitary)</span> An operator $S\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is called unitary if $S$ is an invertible isometry.

- Note that an isometry operator in a finite-dimensional inner product space is always unitary.
- Note that $|\lambda|=1$ for any eigenvalue of $S$.
- A matrix $A\in F^{n\times n}$ is called unitary if its columns form orthonormal basis in $F^n$.

## 1.5. Singular Value Decomposition

### 1.5.1. <span style="color: red">(Singular value)</span> Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V,W)$. The singular values of $T$ are nonnegative square roots of the eigenvalues of $T^*T$, listed in decreasing order, each included as many times as the dimension of the corresponding eigenspace of $T^*T$.

## 1.6. Consequences of Singular Value Decomposition

### 1.6.1. <span style="color: red">(Norm of a linear map)</span> Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V,W)$. Then the norm of $T$, denoted by $\|T\|$ is defined by

$$
\|T\|=\sup\{\|Tv\|:v \in V\ and\ \|v\| \leq 1\}.
$$

# 2. Theorems

## 2.1. Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators

### 2.1.1. Suppose $U,V,W$ are finite-dimensional inner product spaces, $T_1,T_2\in\mathcal L(U,V)$, $\lambda\in F$, and let $S\in\mathcal{L}(V,W),\ T\in\mathcal{L}(U,V)$. Then

(1) $(T_1+T_2)^*=T_1^*+T_2^*$

(2) $(\lambda T)^*=\overline{\lambda}T^*$

(3) $\big(T^*\big)^*=T$

(4) $\big( ST \big)^*=T^* S^*$

(5) $I^*=I$

(6) If $T$ is invertible, then $T^*$ is invertible and $\big(T^{-1}\big)^*=\big(T^*\big)^{-1}$

### 2.1.2. Suppose $V,W$ are finite-dimensional inner product spaces and let $T\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)$. Then

(1) $\ker(T^*)=(\operatorname{range}(T))^{\perp}$

(2) $\operatorname{range}(T^*)=(\ker(T))^{\perp}$

(3) $\ker(T)=\big(\operatorname{range}(T^*)\big)^{\perp}$

(4) $\operatorname{range}(T)=\big(\ker(T^*)\big)^{\perp}$

- Note that $\operatorname{rank}(T)=\operatorname{rank}(T^*)$.

### 2.1.3. Suppose $V,W$ are finite-dimensional inner product spaces and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)$. Suppose $\beta=\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\},\gamma=\{f_1,\ldots,f_m\}$ are orthonormal bases of $V$ and $W$. Then

$$
\big[T^*\big]_{\gamma}^{\beta}=\Big(\big[T\big]_{\beta}^{\gamma}\Big)^*
$$

- Let $U$ be a subspace of a finite-dimensional inner product space $V$ and let $R:V \rightarrow V',\ R(v)=\varphi_v$ where $\varphi_v(u)=\langle u,v \rangle$ in Riesz representation theorem. Then $U^0=R(U^{\perp})$

### 2.1.4. Suppose $V$ is a complex inner product space and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Then

(1) $\langle Tv,v \rangle=0\text{ for every }v\in V \iff T=0$

(2) $T\text{ is self-adjoint} \iff \langle Tv, v \rangle \in \mathbb{R}\text{ for every }v\in V$

- Note the property (1) does not hold if $F=\mathbb{R}$.

### 2.1.5. Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is self-adjoint. Then

(1) Every eigenvalue of $T$ is real

(2) $\langle Tv,v \rangle=0\text{ for every }v\in V \iff T=0$

### 2.1.6. Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Then

$$
T \text{ is normal}\\
\iff \|Tv\|=\|T^*v\|\text{ for every }v\in V\\
\overset{F=\mathbb{C}}{\iff}T=A+iB \text{ for some commuting self-adjoint }A,B\\
\overset{F=\mathbb{C}}{\iff} \exists\ p \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C})\text{ such that }T^*=p(T)
$$

### 2.1.7. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional inner product space and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is normal. Then

(1) $\ker(T)=\ker(T^*)$

(2) $\operatorname{range}(T)=\operatorname{range}(T^*)$

(3) $V=\ker(T)\oplus \operatorname{range}(T)$

(4) $T-\lambda I$ is normal for every $\lambda\in F$

(5) If $v\in V$ and $\lambda\in F$, then $Tv=\lambda v$ if and only if $T^*v=\overline{\lambda} v$

(6) Eigenvectors of $T$ corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal

## 2.2. Spectral Theorem

### 2.2.1. Suppose $T\in \mathcal{L}(V)$ is self-adjoint and $b,c\in\mathbb R$ are such that $b^2 < 4c$. Then

$$
T^2+bT+cI
$$

is an invertible operator.

Proof sketch. For $v\ne0$, $\langle(T^2+bT+cI)v,v\rangle=\|(T+\frac b2 I)v\|^2+(c-\frac{b^2}{4})\|v\|^2>0$.

### 2.2.2. Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ is self-adjoint. Then the minimal polynomial of $T$ equals $(z-\lambda_1)\cdots(z-\lambda_m)$ for some distinct $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m \in \mathbb{R}$.

Proof sketch. Self-adjoint operators have real eigenvalues. Over $\mathbb R$, an irreducible quadratic factor $z^2+bz+c$ satisfies $b^2<4c$, so §2.2.1 makes $T^2+bT+cI$ invertible; such a factor cannot occur in the minimal polynomial. Finally, self-adjointness rules out repeated linear factors: $\ker((T-\lambda I)^2)=\ker(T-\lambda I)$.

### 2.2.3. <span style="color: red">(Real spectral theorem)</span> Suppose $F=\mathbb{R}$ and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1) $T$ is self-adjoint

(2) $T$ has a diagonal matrix with respect to some orthonormal basis of $V$

(3) $V$ has an orthonormal basis consisting of eigenvectors of $T$

### 2.2.4. <span style="color: red">(Complex spectral theorem)</span> Suppose $F=\mathbb{C}$ and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1) $T$ is normal

(2) $T$ has a diagonal matrix with respect to some orthonormal basis of $V$

(3) $V$ has an orthonormal basis consisting of eigenvectors of $T$

## 2.3. Positive Operators

### 2.3.1. Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional inner product space and let $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1) $T$ is a positive operator.

(2) $T$ is self-adjoint and all eigenvalues of $T$ are nonnegative.

(3) With respect to some orthonormal basis of $V$, the matrix of $T$ is a diagonal matrix with only nonnegative numbers on the diagonal.

(4) $T$ has a positive square root

(5) $T$ has a self-adjoint square root

(6) $T=R^*R$ for some $R \in \mathcal{L}(V)$

- Note that each positive operator on $V$ has a unique positive square root.

### 2.3.2. Suppose $T$ is a positive operator on $V$ and $v\in V$ is such that $\langle Tv,v \rangle=0$. Then $Tv=0$.

## 2.4. Isometrics, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization

### 2.4.1. Suppose $V,W$ are finite-dimensional inner product spaces and let $S \in \mathcal{L}(V,W)$. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1) $S$ is an isometry

(2) $S^*S=I$

(3) $\langle Su, Sv \rangle = \langle u,v \rangle$ for all $u,v\in V$

(4) $Se_1,\ldots,Se_n$ is an orthonormal list in $W$ for an orthonormal basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ of $V$

(5) Suppose $\beta,\gamma$ are orthonormal bases of $V,W$, respectively. Then the columns of $[S]_\beta^\gamma$ form an orthonormal list in $F^{\dim W}$ with respect to the Euclidean inner product.

### 2.4.2. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional inner product space with an orthonormal basis $\beta=\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\}$ and let $S\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1) $S$ is a unitary operator

(2) $S^*S=SS^*=I$

(3) $S$ is invertible and $S^{-1}=S^*$

(4) $Se_1,\ldots,Se_n$ is an orthonormal basis of $V$

(5) The rows of $[S]_{\beta}$ form an orthonormal basis of $F^n$ with respect to the Euclidean inner product

(6) $S^*$ is a unitary operator

### 2.4.3. Suppose $F=\mathbb{C}$ and $S \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1) $S$ is a unitary operator

(2) There is an orthonormal basis of $V$ consisting of eigenvectors of $S$, whose corresponding eigenvalues all have absolute value 1

### 2.4.4. Suppose $Q \in F^{n \times n}$. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1) $Q$ is a unitary matrix

(2) The rows of $Q$ form an orthonormal basis of $F^n$

(3) $\|Qv\|=\|v\|$ for every $v \in F^n$

(4) $Q^*Q=QQ^*=I$

### 2.4.5. <span style="color: red">(QR factorization)</span> Suppose $A\in F^{n\times n}$ has linearly independent columns. Then there exist unique matrices $Q\text{(unitary)},R\text{(upper triangular with positive diagonal entries)} \in F^{n \times n}$ such that

$$
A=QR.
$$

- The columns of $Q$ can be computed by applying Gram-Schmidt procedure to the columns of $A$.

### 2.4.6. <span style="color: red">(Cholesky factorization)</span> Suppose $B$ is a positive definite matrix. Then there exists a unique upper-triangular matrix $R$ with only positive numbers on its diagonal such that

$$
B=R^*R.
$$

## 2.5. Singular Value Decomposition

### 2.5.1. Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V,W)$. Then

(1) $T^*T$ is a positive operator on $V$

(2) $\ker(T^*T)=\ker(T)$

(3) $\operatorname{range}(T^*T)=\operatorname{range}(T^*)=(\ker(T))^\perp$

(4) $\operatorname{rank}(T^*T)=\operatorname{rank}(T^*)=\operatorname{rank}(T)$

### 2.5.2. Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V,W)$. Then

(1) $T$ is injective $\iff$ $0$ is not a singular value of $T$

(2) $T$ is surjective $\iff$ the number of positive singular values of $T$ equals $\dim(W)$

(3) The number of positive singular values of $T$ equals $\operatorname{rank}(T)$

(4) $T$ is an isometry $\iff$ all singular values of $T$ equal 1

### 2.5.3. <span style="color: red">(Singular value decomposition)</span> Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V,W)$ and the positive singular values of $T$ are $s_1,\ldots,s_m$, counted with multiplicity ($m=\operatorname{rank}T$). Then there exist orthonormal lists $e_1,\ldots,e_m$ in $V$ and $f_1,\ldots,f_m$ in $W$ where

$$
f_k=\cfrac{Te_k}{s_k},\ k=1,\ldots,m
$$

such that

$$
Tv=s_1 \langle v,e_1 \rangle f_1 + \cdots +s_m \langle v,e_m \rangle f_m\\
T^*w=s_1\langle w,f_1 \rangle e_1+\cdots+s_m\langle w,f_m \rangle e_m\\
T^{\dagger}w=\cfrac{\langle w,f_1 \rangle}{s_1}e_1 + \cdots + \cfrac{\langle w,f_m \rangle}{s_m}e_m
$$

for every $v \in V$ and $w \in W$. If $T=0$, take $m=0$ and interpret all three sums as zero.

### 2.5.4. <span style="color: red">(SVD for matrix)</span> Suppose $A \in F^{p \times n}$ of rank $m \geq 1$. Then there exist $B \in F^{p \times m}$ with orthonormal columns, a diagonal matrix with positive entries $D \in F^{m \times m}$, and $C \in F^{n \times m}$ with orthonormal columns such that

$$
A=BDC^*.
$$

## 2.6. Consequences of Singular Value Decomposition

### 2.6.1. Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V,W)$. Let $s_1$ be the largest singular value of $T$. Then

$$
\|T\| = s_1
$$

for $V\ne\{0\}$. If $V=\{0\}$, then $\|T\|=0$.

- Note that $\|Tv\| \leq \|T\|\|v\|$ and $\|T\|$ is the smallest bound.

### 2.6.2. Suppose $S,T \in \mathcal{L}(V,W)$. Then

(1) $\|T\| \geq 0$

(2) $\|T\|=0 \iff T=0$

(3) $\|\lambda T\|=|\lambda|\|T\|$ for all $\lambda \in F$

(4) $\|S + T\| \leq \|S\|+\|T\|$

(5) $\|T^*\|=\|T\|$

### 2.6.3. Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V,W)$ and $s_1 \geq \cdots \geq s_m$ are the positive singular values of $T$ with corresponding orthonormal vectors $e_1,\ldots,e_m$ in $V$ and $f_1,\ldots,f_m$ in $W$. Suppose $1 \leq k < m$. Then

$$
\min\{\|T-S\|:S \in \mathcal{L}(V,W)\text{ and }\operatorname{rank}(S)\leq k\}=s_{k+1}.
$$

Furthermore, $\operatorname{rank}(T_k)=k$ and $\|T-T_k\|=s_{k+1}$ where

$$
T_kv=s_1\langle v,e_1 \rangle f_1 + \cdots + s_k \langle v,e_k \rangle f_k
$$

for every $v \in V$.

### 2.6.4. Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. Then there exists a unitary operator $S \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ such that

$$
T=S\sqrt{T^*T}
$$
