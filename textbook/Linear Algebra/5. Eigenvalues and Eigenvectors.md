# 5. Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Source: https://www.notion.so/40669428c86181299f5ad22adfdda377

> Throughout this chapter, $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space over $F=\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$, and $T\in\mathcal L(V)$. A statement about the existence of an eigenvalue assumes $V\ne\{0\}$. The minimal polynomial of the unique operator on $\{0\}$ is $1$, with empty products interpreted as $1$.

## 1. Definitions

### 1.1. Invariant Subspaces

#### 1.1.1. A linear map from a vector space to itself is called an **operator**.

#### 1.1.2. Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. A subspace $U$ of $V$ is called **invariant** under $T$ if $Tu \in U$ for every $u \in U$.

Note that $U$ is invariant under $T$ if $T|_U$ is an operator on $U$.

#### 1.1.3. Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. A number $\lambda \in F$ is called an **eigenvalue** of $T$ if there exists $v\in V$ such that $v \neq 0$ and $Tv=\lambda v$.

#### 1.1.4. Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and $\lambda\in F$ is an eigenvalue of $T$. A vector $v\in V$ is called an **eigenvector** of $T$ corresponding to $\lambda$ if $v \neq 0$ and $Tv=\lambda v$.

#### 1.1.5. Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and $p\in \mathcal{P}(F)$ is a polynomial given by

$$
p(z)=a_0+a_1z+\ldots+a_mz^m
$$

for all $z\in F$. Then $p(T)$ is the operator on $V$ defined by

$$
p(T)=a_0I+a_1T+\ldots+a_mT^m
$$

where $T^m=\overbrace{T\circ \cdots \circ T}^{m\text{ times}}$, $T^0=I$.

(1) Note that if $T^{-1}$ exists, then $T^{-m}=\big(T^{-1}\big)^m$.

(2) Note that $(pq)(T)=p(T)q(T)=q(T)p(T)$.

#### 1.1.6. Suppose $V$ is finite-dimensional, $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$, and $U$ is a subspace of $V$ invariant under $T$. Then the **quotient operator** $T/U\in\mathcal{L}(V/U)$ is defined by

$$
(T/U)(v+U)=Tv+U,\ \forall v\in V.
$$

- Note that the quotient operator is well-defined because $U$ is invariant under $T$.

### 1.2. The Minimal Polynomial

#### 1.2.1. Suppose $V$ is finite-dimensional and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Then the minimal polynomial of $T$ is the unique monic polynomial $p\in\mathcal{P}(F)$ of smallest degree such that $p(T)=0$.

### 1.3. Upper-Triangular Matrices

A square matrix $A$ is upper triangular if $A_{i,j}=0$ whenever $i>j$.

### 1.4. Diagonalizable Operators

#### 1.4.1. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space. An operator on $V$ is called diagonalizable if the operator has a diagonal matrix with respect to some basis of $V$.

#### 1.4.2. Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ and $\lambda\in F$. The eigenspace of $T$ corresponding to $\lambda$ is the subspace $E(\lambda,T)$ of $V$ defined by

$$
E(\lambda, T)=\ker(T-\lambda I)=\{v\in V: Tv=\lambda v\}
$$

#### 1.4.3. <span style="color: red">(Gershgorin disks)</span> Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$, $\mathscr{B}=\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$ is a basis of $V$, and $A=[T]_{\mathscr{B}}$. A **Gershgorin disk** of $T$ with respect to the basis $\mathscr{B}$ is a set of the form

$$
D_i=\big\{z\in F:|z-A_{i,i}|\leq \sum_{
\begin{subarray}{l}
   j=1\\
   j\neq i
\end{subarray}}^{n}|A_{i,j}|\big\}
$$

### 1.5. Commuting Operators

#### 1.5.1. Two operators $S,T$ on the same vector space are said to **commute** if $ST=TS$.

Two square matrices $A,B$ are said to commute if $AB=BA$.

- Note that for $p,q\in\mathcal{P}(F)$, $p(T)$ and $q(T)$ commute.

## 2. Theorems

### 2.1. Invariant Subspaces

#### 2.1.1. Suppose $V$ is finite-dimensional, $T\in \mathcal{L}(V)$, and $\lambda \in F$. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1) $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $T$.

(2) $T-\lambda I$ is not injective.

(3) $T-\lambda I$ is not surjective.

(4) $T-\lambda I$ is not invertible.

#### 2.1.2. Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Then every list of eigenvectors of $T$ corresponding to distinct eigenvalues of $T$ is linearly independent.

Proof sketch. Induct on the number of eigenvectors. Applying $T-\lambda_mI$ to a linear relation reduces it to a relation among the first $m-1$ eigenvectors; distinctness of the eigenvalues forces every coefficient to vanish.

- Note that if $V$ is finite-dimensional, then each operator on $V$ has at most $\dim(V)$ distinct eigenvalues.

#### 2.1.3. Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ and $p\in\mathcal{P}(F)$. Then $\ker(p(T))$ and $\operatorname{range}(p(T))$ are invariant under $T$.

#### 2.1.4. Suppose $V$ is finite-dimensional, $T\in\mathcal{L}(V),\ \lambda\in F$. Then $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $T$ if and only if $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of the dual operator $T'$.

Proof sketch. $\ker(T'-\lambda I)=(\operatorname{range}(T-\lambda I))^0$. In finite dimensions, this annihilator is nonzero exactly when $T-\lambda I$ is not surjective.

#### 2.1.5. Let $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ and let $m\geq1$ be an integer. Then the following statements hold.

(1) $T$ is injective $\iff$ $T^m$ is injective.

(2) $T$ is surjective $\iff$ $T^m$ is surjective.

#### 2.1.6. Suppose $V$ is finite-dimensional. Then $\beta = \{v_1,\ldots,v_m\}$ is linearly independent if and only if there exists $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ such that $\beta$ is a set of eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues.

Note that we can determine the linear independence by finding such $T$.

Example. On $\operatorname{span}(e^{\lambda_1x},\ldots,e^{\lambda_kx})$, the differentiation operator $D$ has these nonzero functions as eigenvectors with distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k$. Hence they are linearly independent.

### 2.2. The Minimal Polynomial

#### 2.2.1. Every operator on a finite-dimensional nonzero complex vector space has an eigenvalue.

Proof sketch. For $v\ne0$ and $n=\dim V$, the list $v,Tv,\ldots,T^nv$ is linearly dependent. Hence $p(T)v=0$ for a nonzero polynomial $p$. Factor $p$ over $\mathbb C$; if every $T-\lambda I$ were injective, their product would be injective, contradicting $p(T)v=0$.

#### 2.2.2. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Then there is a unique monic polynomial $p\in\mathcal{P}(F)$ of smallest degree such that $p(T)=0$. Furthermore, $\deg(p) \leq \dim(V)$.

#### 2.2.3. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Then

(1) The zeros of the minimal polynomial of $T$ are all the eigenvalues of $T$.

(2) If $V$ is a complex vector space, then the minimal polynomial of $T$ has the form

$$
(z-\lambda_1)\cdots (z-\lambda_m),
$$

where $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m$ is a list of all the eigenvalues of $T$, possibly with repetitions.

#### 2.2.4. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space, $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$, and $q\in\mathcal{P}(F)$. Then $q(T)=0$ if and only if $q$ is a polynomial multiple of the minimal polynomial of $T$.

#### 2.2.5. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space, $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$, $q\in\mathcal{P}(F)$, and $U$ is a subspace of $V$ that is invariant under $T$. Then the minimal polynomial of $T$ is a polynomial multiple of the minimal polynomial of $T|_{U}$.

#### 2.2.6. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Then $T$ is not invertible if and only if the constant term of the minimal polynomial of $T$ is zero.

Proof sketch. $T$ is not invertible if and only if $0$ is an eigenvalue of $T$.

#### 2.2.7. Suppose $F=\mathbb{C}$, $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$, $p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C})$ is a nonconstant polynomial, and $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$. Then $\alpha$ is an eigenvalue of $p(T)$ if and only if $\alpha=p(\lambda)$ for some eigenvalue of $T$.

#### 2.2.8. Every operator on an odd-dimensional vector space has an eigenvalue, even if $F=\mathbb{R}$.

### 2.3. Upper-Triangular Matrices

#### 2.3.1. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space with a basis $\mathscr{B}=\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$ and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Then

$$
\begin{bmatrix}T\end{bmatrix}_{\mathscr{B}}\text{ is upper triangular} \\
\iff \operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_k)\text{ is invariant under }T\text{ for each }k=1,\ldots,n
$$

(1) The matrix of $T$ with respect to $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ is upper triangular.

(2) $\operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_k)$ is invariant under $T$ for each $k=1,\ldots,n$.

(3) $Tv_k\in \operatorname{span}\{v_1,\ldots,v_k\}$ for each $k=1,\ldots,n$.

#### 2.3.2. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space with a basis with respect to which $T$ has an upper triangular matrix with diagonal entries $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$, then

(1)  $(T-\lambda_1 I)\cdots(T-\lambda_n I)=0$

- Note that every polynomial that makes $T$ the zero operator is the multiple of the minimal polynomial.
  - If $q(T)=0$ and $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue, then $q(\lambda)=0$.

- For each diagonal entry $\lambda_k$, the matrix of $T-\lambda_kI$ is triangular with a zero diagonal entry and is therefore not invertible. Thus $\lambda_k$ is an eigenvalue.

(2) $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$ are precisely the eigenvalues of $T$.

#### 2.3.3. Suppose $V$ is finite-dimensional and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Then $T$ has an upper triangular matrix with respect to some basis of $V$ if and only if the minimal polynomial of $T$ equals $(z-\lambda_1)\cdots(z-\lambda_m)$ for some $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m\in F$, possibly repeated.

- Note that if $F=\mathbb{C}$, then $T$ always has an upper-triangular matrix with respect to some basis of $V$.

### 2.4. Diagonalizable Operators

#### 2.4.1. Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ and $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m$ are distinct eigenvalues of $T$. Then

$$
E(\lambda_1,T)+\cdots+E(\lambda_m,T)
$$

is a direct sum. Furthermore, if $V$ is finite-dimensional, then

$$
\dim E(\lambda_1,T)+\cdots+\dim E(\lambda_m,T) \leq \dim(V).
$$

Note that $\dim(V_1)+\cdots+\dim(V_n)=\dim(V_1\oplus\cdots \oplus V_n)$

#### 2.4.2. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Let $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m$ denote the distinct eigenvalues of $T$. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1) $T$ is diagonalizable.

(2) $V$ has a basis consisting of eigenvectors of $T$.

(3) $V=E(\lambda_1,T)\oplus\cdots\oplus E(\lambda_m,T)$

(4) $\dim(V)=\dim(E(\lambda_1,T))+\cdots+\dim(E(\lambda_m,T))$.

- Note that if there are $\dim(V)$ distinct eigenvalues, then $T$ is diagonalizable (not vice versa).

#### 2.4.3. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Then $T$ is diagonalizable if and only if the minimal polynomial of $T$ equals $(z-\lambda_1)\cdots(z-\lambda_m)$ for distinct $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m\in F$.

- Note that idempotent operators are always diagonalizable.

#### 2.4.4. Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is diagonalizable and $U$ is a subspace of $V$ that is invariant under $T$. Then $T|_U$ is a diagonalizable operator on $U$.

#### 2.4.5. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space, $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ and $\mathscr{B}$ is a basis of $V$. Then each eigenvalue of $T$ is contained in at least one Gershgorin disk of $T$ with respect to the basis $\mathscr{B}$.

### 2.5. Commuting Operators

#### 2.5.1. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space, $S,T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$, and $\mathscr{B}$ is a basis of $V$. Then $S$ and $T$ commute if and only if $[S]_{\mathscr{B}}$ and $[T]_{\mathscr{B}}$ commute.

#### 2.5.2. Suppose $S,T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ commute and $\lambda\in F$. Then $E(\lambda,S)$ is invariant under $T$.

#### 2.5.3. Two diagonalizable operators on the same finite-dimensional vector space have diagonal matrices with respect to the same basis if and only if the two operators commute.

#### 2.5.4. Every pair of commuting operators on a finite-dimensional nonzero complex vector space has a common eigenvector.

Proof sketch. Choose an eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $S$. The nonzero space $E(\lambda,S)$ is invariant under $T$; an eigenvector of $T|_{E(\lambda,S)}$ is a common eigenvector.

#### 2.5.5. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional complex vector space and $S,T$ are commuting operators on $V$. Then there is a basis with respect to which both $S$ and $T$ have upper-triangular matrices.

Proof sketch. Choose a common eigenvector $v\ne0$ and write $V=\operatorname{span}(v)\oplus W$. For the projection $P(cv+w)=w$, the induced maps $PS|_W$ and $PT|_W$ commute. Induction gives a common triangularizing basis of $W$; prepend $v$.

#### 2.5.6. Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional complex vector space and $S,T$ are commuting operators on $V$. Then

(1) Every eigenvalue of $S+T$ is an eigenvalue of $S$ plus an eigenvalue of $T$.

(2) Every eigenvalue of $ST$ is an eigenvalue of $S$ times an eigenvalue of $T$.
