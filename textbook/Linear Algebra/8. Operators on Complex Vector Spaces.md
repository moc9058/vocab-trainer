# 8. Operators on Complex Vector Spaces

Source: https://www.notion.so/40669428c86181b591fedd56d0cfe7ba

> Throughout this chapter, $V$ is a nonzero finite-dimensional vector space over $F=\mathbb C$, $n=\dim V$, and $T\in\mathcal L(V)$. The results on kernels of powers, nilpotent operators, and traces also hold over $\mathbb R$; conclusions requiring splitting into eigenvalues use $\mathbb C$. Multiplicity means algebraic multiplicity, equal to the dimension of the generalized eigenspace.

## 1. Definitions

### 1.1. Operators on Complex Vector Spaces

#### 1.1.1. <span style="color: red">(Generalized eigenvector)</span> Suppose $T\in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and $\lambda$<span style="color: blue"><u>** is an eigenvalue**</u></span> of $T$. A vector $v \in V$ is called a generalized eigenvector of $T$ corresponding to $\lambda$ if $v \neq 0$ and

$$
(T-\lambda I)^kv=0
$$

for some positive integer $k$.

#### 1.1.2. <span style="color: red">(Nilpotent)</span> An operator is called nilpotent if some power of it equals $0$.

### 1.2. Generalized Eigenspace Decomposition

#### 1.2.1. <span style="color: red">(Generalized eigenspace)</span> Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V), \dim(V)=n$ and $\lambda \in F$. The generalized eigenspace corresponding to $\lambda$ is defined by

$$
G(\lambda, T)=\{v \in V:(T- \lambda I)^kv=0\ \text{for some positive integer}\ k\}.
$$

- Obviously it is a subspace of $V$

- $G(\lambda, T)=\ker((T-\lambda I)^n)$

#### 1.2.2. <span style="color: red">(Multiplicity)</span> Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and $\dim(V)=n$. The multiplicity of an eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $T$ is defined as the following:

$$
multiplicity(\lambda, T)=\operatorname{nullity}((T-\lambda I)^n)=\dim(G(\lambda, T)).
$$

#### 1.2.3. <span style="color: red">(Characteristic polynomial)</span> Suppose $T\in \mathcal{L}(V),\ \dim(V)=n$ and $F=\mathbb{C}$. Let $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m$ denote the distinct eigenvalues of $T$ with multiplicities $d_1,\ldots,d_m$. Then the following polynomial is called the characteristic polynomial of $T$.

$$
(z-\lambda_1)^{d_1}\cdots(z-\lambda_m)^{d_m}
$$

- The generalized eigenspace decomposition gives $d_1+\cdots+d_m=n$.

### 1.3. Consequences of Generalized Eigenspace Decomposition

#### 1.3.1. <span style="color: red">(Jordan basis)</span> Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. A basis $\beta$ of $V$ is called Jordan basis for $T$ if

$$
[T]_\beta=\begin{pmatrix}
A_1 &  & 0 \\
  & \ddots & \\
0 &  & A_p
\end{pmatrix}
$$

in which each $A_k$ is an upper triangular matrix of the form

$$
A_k=J_{r_k}(\lambda_k),\qquad
(J_r(\lambda))_{i,j}=
\begin{cases}
\lambda & i=j,\\
1 & j=i+1,\\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

### 1.4. Trace: A Connection Between Matrices and Operators

#### 1.4.1. <span style="color: red">(Trace)</span> Suppose $A\in F^{n \times n}$. Then

$$
\operatorname{tr}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^n A_{i,i}
$$

Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ with $\dim(V)=n$. Then

$$
\operatorname{tr}(T)=\operatorname{tr}([T]_\beta)
$$

## 2. Theorems

### 2.1. Operators on Complex Vector Spaces

#### 2.1.1. Suppose $T\in \mathcal{L}(V),\ \dim(V)=n$. Then

(1) $\ker(T^m)=\ker(T^{m+1})$ implies $\ker(T^m)=\ker(T^{m+k})$ for every integer $k\geq0$, where $m\geq0$

- Especially, $\ker(T^n)=\ker(T^{n+k})$ for every integer $k\geq0$.

(2) $V=\ker(T^n) \oplus \operatorname{range}(T^n)$

#### 2.1.2. Suppose $F = \mathbb{C}$ and $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. Then there is a basis of $V$ consisting of generalized eigenvectors of $T$.

#### 2.1.3. Suppose $T\in \mathcal{L}(V)$. Then the following statements hold.

(1) Each generalized eigenvector of $T$ corresponds to only one eigenvalue of $T$

(2) Every list of generalized eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues of $T$ is linearly independent

#### 2.1.4. Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V),\ \dim(V)=n$. Then the following statements hold.

(1) If $T$ is nilpotent, then $0$ is an eigenvalue of $T$ and $T$ has no other eigenvalues

(2) If $T$ is nilpotent, then $T^n=0$ (and $\operatorname{range}T^n=\{0\}$)

(3) If $F=\mathbb{C}$ and $0$ is only eigenvalue of $T$, then $T$ is nilpotent.

#### 2.1.5. Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V),\ \dim(V)=n$. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1) $T$ is nilpotent

(2) The minimal polynomial of $T$ is $z^m$ for some positive integer $m$

(3) There is a basis of $V$ with respect to which the matrix of $T$ has the form

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
0 &  & *\\
  & \ddots &  \\
0 &  & 0
\end{pmatrix}
$$

(4) $T^n=0$

### 2.2. Generalized Eigenspace Decomposition

#### 2.2.1. Suppose $F=\mathbb{C}$ and $T\in \mathcal{L}(V)$. Let $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m$ be the distinct eigenvalues of $T$. Then

$$
V=G(\lambda_1,T) \oplus \cdots \oplus G(\lambda_m,T)
$$

- The sum of the multiplicities of all eigenvalues of T equals $\dim(V)$.

#### 2.2.2. <span style="color: red">(Cayley-Hamilton theorem)</span> Suppose $F=\mathbb{C},\ T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and $q$ is the characteristic polynomial of $T$. Then $q(T)=0$.

- Note that the characteristic polynomial of $T$ is a polynomial multiple of the minimal polynomial of $T$.

- More generally, if the minimal polynomial is $z^kp(z)$ with $k\geq1$ and $p(0)\ne0$, then $V=\ker(T^k)\oplus\ker(p(T))$. This follows from the coprimality of $z^k$ and $p$.

#### 2.2.3. Suppose $F = \mathbb{C}, T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and $\dim(V)=n$. Suppose $\beta = \{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$ is a basis of $V$ such that $[T]_\beta$ is upper triangular. Then the number of times that each eigenvalue $\lambda$ appears on the diagonal element equals the multiplicity of it.

- The number of appearance of the eigenvalue = Dimension of the generalized eigenspace

- The exponent of $(z-\lambda)$ in the minimal polynomial is the smallest positive integer $r$ for which $(T-\lambda I)^r$ vanishes on $G(\lambda,T)$; it is the size of the largest Jordan block for $\lambda$.

#### 2.2.4. Suppose $F = \mathbb{C}, T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and $\dim(V)=n$. Let $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m$ be the distinct eigenvalues of $T$ with multiplicities $d_1,\ldots,d_m$. There exists a basis $\beta$ where

$$
[T]_\beta
=\begin{pmatrix}
A_1 &  & 0\\
  & \ddots &  \\
0 &  &  A_m
\end{pmatrix},\ \\
A_k=\begin{pmatrix}
\lambda_k &  & *\\
  & \ddots &  \\
0 &  &  \lambda_k
\end{pmatrix} \in F^{d_k \times d_k}.
$$

### 2.3. Consequences of Generalized Eigenspace Decomposition

#### 2.3.1. Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is nilpotent. Then $I+T$ has a square root.

- A square root is $R=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\binom{1/2}{j}T^j$, where $m\geq1$ is the least integer with $T^m=0$. The truncated binomial identity gives $R^2=I+T$.

#### 2.3.2. Suppose $V$ is a complex vector space and $T\in \mathcal{L}(V)$ is invertible. Then $T$ has a square root.

#### 2.3.3. Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is nilpotent. Then there is a basis of $V$ that is a Jordan basis.

- Note that there exists a Jordan basis if $F=\mathbb{C}$

- For a nilpotent operator, the number of Jordan blocks equals $\dim\ker T$. More generally, the number of blocks for $\lambda$ equals $\dim\ker(T-\lambda I)$; their sizes are determined by the dimensions $\dim\ker((T-\lambda I)^j)$ and are independent of the chosen Jordan basis.

### 2.4. Trace: A Connection Between Matrices and Operators

#### 2.4.1. Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V), \dim(V)=n$ and let $\beta,\gamma$ are bases of $V$. Then

$$
\operatorname{tr}([T]_\beta)=\operatorname{tr}([T]_\gamma)
$$

#### 2.4.2. Suppose $F=\mathbb{C}$ and $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ with distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m$, whose multiplicities are $d_1,\ldots,d_m$. Then

$$
\operatorname{tr}(T)=d_1\lambda_1 + \cdots + d_m\lambda_m
$$

- Note that $\operatorname{tr}(T)$ equals the negative of the coefficient of $z^{n-1}$ in the characteristic polynomial of $T$

- Note that $\operatorname{tr}(T)=\langle Te_1,e_1 \rangle + \cdots + \langle Te_n,e_n \rangle$ for orthonormal basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$

- For $T\in\mathcal L(V,W)$ and orthonormal bases $e_1,\ldots,e_m$ of $V$ and $f_1,\ldots,f_n$ of $W$,

$$
\operatorname{tr}(T^*T)=\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^n|\langle Te_i,f_j\rangle|^2
$$

#### 2.4.3. The function $\operatorname{tr}:\mathcal{L}(V)\rightarrow F$ is a linear functional on $\mathcal{L}(V)$ such that

$$
\operatorname{tr}(ST)=\operatorname{tr}(TS)
$$

for all $S,T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$.

- These statements concern finite-dimensional spaces. A trace of arbitrary operators on an infinite-dimensional space is not defined by this construction.

- $tr$ is the only linear functional that satisfies $\operatorname{tr}(ST)=\operatorname{tr}(TS)$ and $\operatorname{tr}(I)=\dim(V)$

#### 2.4.4. There do not exist operators $S,T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ such that $ST-TS=I$.
